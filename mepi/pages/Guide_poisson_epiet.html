MVA - Multivariable analysis

Case study: HIV Sentinel System in Spain - Poisson regression

Objectives of this case study

At the end of the case study, participants should be able to:

  • perform Poisson regression and interpret the results,
  • identify overdispersion and use appropriate models to address it,
  • perform negative binomial regression and interpret the model

Previous level of expertise assumed

Participants are expected to be familiar with data import, management as well as descriptive and linear/logistic regression in R.

Preparation for the case study

  1. Extract the contents of the folder named poisson_mva in the local laptop

  2. Create an Rstudio project in the folder poisson_mva. If you are unsure on how to do that, read the EpiRhandbook Chapter on R projects

  3. Inside the folder poisson_mva, the subfolder “data” contains the data needed for this case study: HIV_Spain.dta. There is another file named data2005.txt which will be used for some optional tasks at the end.

  4. Subfolder scripts should be used to save any scripts related to the analysis. Inside “backup” you will find a solution script with the code of the case study named poisson_analysis_backup.R.

  5. Subfolder “outputs” could be used to store all outputs (tables, graphs, documents) that are the result of the analysis.

Scenario, study design and analysis plan

Introduction

In Spain, HIV infection is monitored through a sentinel network of eleven HIV clinics specialising in counselling and HIV testing. These clinics have been operating without major changes since the beginning of the 90s, and epidemiological information and results of HIV tests have been collected in a standardised way

This case study uses information for 1989–2004 on a) people who seroconverted (persons with a recorded HIV-negative test followed by a positive one) b) people who did not seroconvert (had one or more negative test, never testing positive).

Even though people may have been tested several times, information only on the first negative HIV test, the last negative HIV test and the first positive HIV test (where applicable) are shown in the database.

Seroconverters are defined as persons with any negative test followed by a positive one (for them, the variable seroco is equal to 1 ). CD4 counts (cd4) at the time of the first positive test were also recorded.

Healthcare workers collect epidemiological information (self-reported risk behaviours including injecting drug use, condomless sex between men, sex work and condomless heterosexual sex) from participants using a brief questionnaire before an HIV test is performed.

Each participant can report more than one risk category, which is subsequently recoded in separate variables: person who injects drugs (idu), man who has condomless sex with men (msm), sexworker (sexwork) and person with condomless heterosexual contacts (hete).

Your task

You are one of the epidemiologists in charge of the Spanish sentinel surveillance system and you have been asked to study and model the number of HIV infections within this system.

Step 4: Poisson regression

To this point, you have already compared the risks of seroconversion for different exposures and assessed which groups within the population have the highest risk of seroconversion.

By now, you may have realised that sex, age, the different modes of transmission, the year when the test was performed, and the centre region are statistically associated with HIV seroconversion. Through stratified analysis, it can be concluded that some of these variables have an impact on the estimate of the others. In order to investigate several risk factors simultaneously and assess for confounding and effect modification, we need to undertake multivariable analysis.

As you know, the type of regression that we should choose depends on the nature of our data. In this case, our event of interest -or dependent variable- is seroconversion (remember that this is when a person has a recorded HIV-negative test followed by a positive one) which is a binary variable. We saw earlier on that logistic regression is the appropriate method when we have this type of dependent variable. This is true. However, in our database we also have the dates for the different tests. Having time-to-event data gives us the possibility to model the rates of seroconversion. The incidence rate (or incidence density) is the number of incident cases divided by the person-time at risk. In this specific sentinel surveillance system, the numerator is the number of seroconverters, and the denominator is the total person-time at risk, i.e.:

  • the time between the first HIV test (by definition negative in this database) and the first HIV positive test for seroconverters, OR

  • the time between the first HIV test and the last HIV negative test for non-seroconverters).

A regression method that allows to take into account varying exposure times to model incidence rates is Poisson regression, which we will use in this case.

Often, more than one method is appropriate for the same data. In this case, a Cox Proportional Hazards Regression would also be appropriate. We will cover this method in a separate case study.

4.1: Data preparation for poisson regression

In order to take time-to-event information into account we first need to create a variable with the time at risk for each observation. Also, currently, our dependent variable seroco is of class() factor, but given that Poisson regression is defined for count outcomes, we need to transform it into numeric.

Tasks

  • Create a variable named seroco_num which is the numeric version of seroco.

  • Create a variable named timeatrisk with the number of years at risk for each observation in the dataset

Click to read a hint


You should be able to transform seroco into numeric without any issues, but if you struggle to remember how, have a look at the EpiRhandbook section on Converting column class. The time at risk will depend on whether the person seroconverted or didn’t. For those who seroconverted we can calculate the number of days at risk as the difference between the first positive test (first_pos) and the first test they carried out (first_test). For those who did not serconvert the number of days at risk is the difference between the last negative test (last_neg) and the first test they carried out (first_test). In order to transform this substraction into a number we can place it inside the function as.double(). The result will be the number of days at risk. If we want to transform this into years at risk, we can divide it by 365.25. For more information have a look at the EpiRhandbook section on Observation time

As we did with logistic regression, we can use the ‘generalized linear models’ or glm() function to perform poisson regression regression in R. Its basic syntax is: glm([OUTCOME] ~ [COVARIATES]). glm stands for ‘generalized linear models’ and can handle a range of different regression models. There are two different aspects in the poisson regression formula with respect to the logistic regression one. The first is the family = argument. In Poisson regression we should write this argument as:

glm([OUTCOME] ~ [PREDICTOR], family = poisson(link = "log"))

By saying that the family is “poisson” we are saying that our outcome is a count and that the counts follow a Poisson distribution, which is appropriate for rare events occurring independently over a fixed period or space. The ‘link = “log”’ part means that the expected value of the dependent variable will be transformed using the natural logarithm. This has several important purposes such as ensuring non-negative predictions or ensuring that the relationship between the predictors and the log of the expected count is linear, which simplifies interpretation and estimation.

The second thing we need to ensure in Poisson regression is that we add an offset = argument. The offset is used to account for varying exposure times among individuals (timeatrisk). It adjusts the model to ensure that the rate of the event (seroconversion) is correctly estimated, rather than just the count. The offset is typically included on the log scale, which aligns with the log link function used in Poisson regression, so it would look as: offset = log(timeatrisk) in our formula.

Finally, to interpret the result you can use the tidy() function, which we have used in previous case studies. Ensure that you add the arguments exponentiate = TRUE and conf.int = TRUE so that the results are transformed from logarithm scale into the orginal scale and they are presented with their confidence interval.

Step 5: Assumptions of Poisson regression models

The regression model we built comes, like any other statistical model, with a set of assumptions that need to be met for the results to be valid. The main asssumptions of regression models are:

  • Independence of observations: The observations are independent of each other, which means that the occurrence of an event for one individual should not influence the occurrence of an event for another individual.

  • Log-linear relationship: The log of the expected value of the outcome variable is a linear combination of the predictor (or independent) variables.

  • No excessive Zero counts: The model assumes that the number of zero counts is consistent with the Poisson distribution. If there are more zeros than expected (zero inflation), a zero-inflated Poisson model might be more appropriate.

  • The outcome variable follows a Poisson distribution: The outcome variable (in our case the number of seroconversions) follows a Poisson distribution. This implies that the mean and variance of this variable are equal. If the variance is much larger than the mean (overdispersion), the model may not fit well.

Frequently, the real-life data that we use in epidemiology does not fulfill this last assumption (that the outcome variable follows a Poisson distribution). This is important because a Poisson model is not valid if overdispersion (or underdispersion) exists. If overdispersion is present, we can consider running alternative models such as Quasi-Poisson regression or Negative Binomial regression. The Negative Binomial regression includes an extra parameter that allows testing and compensating for overdispersion.

# For each combination of year, sex, age2, and idu, calculate:
# - 'atrisk': Number of people at risk for seroconversion in that year
# - 'seroconversions': Number of seroconversions in that year
agg_db <- A %>%
  rowwise() %>% # Process each row independently (needed for custom calculations)
  mutate(
    # Calculate the number of people at risk
    atrisk = sum(
      year(hiv.data$first_test) <= year &  # Tested for the first time on or before the current year
        hiv.data$lasttestY <= year &      # Last test year is on or before the current year
        hiv.data$sex == sex &             # Matches the current 'sex' level
        hiv.data$age2 == age2 &           # Matches the current 'age2' level
        hiv.data$idu == idu               # Matches the current 'idu' level
    ) -
      sum(
        hiv.data$sex == sex &               # Matches the current 'sex' level
          hiv.data$age2 == age2 &           # Matches the current 'age2' level
          hiv.data$idu == idu &             # Matches the current 'idu' level
          hiv.data$first_pos <= year + 1,   # First positive test is before or during the next year
        na.rm = TRUE                        # Ignore missing values
      ),
    
    # Calculate the number of seroconversions
    seroconversions = sum(
      year(hiv.data$first_pos) == year &  # First positive test is in the current year
        hiv.data$sex == sex &             # Matches the current 'sex' level
        hiv.data$age2 == age2 &           # Matches the current 'age2' level
        hiv.data$idu == idu,              # Matches the current 'idu' level
      na.rm = TRUE                        # Ignore missing values
    )
  ) %>%
  ungroup()  %>% 
  
  filter(atrisk > 0) # This step removes combinations where 'atrisk' is 0 or less

Conclusions

The HIV sentinel surveillance system developed in Spain enabled analysis of factors associated with HIV infection risk in the Spanish high-risk population. Based on this system, interventions can be better planned to reduce risk of infection in this population.

The included data were based on the Sentinel Surveillance system between 1989 and 2004, although they were modified for training purposes; they provide information on 21,616 persons, mostly of males (58%), with a mean age of 31.1 years. Of those who had the information on HIV transmission category recorded, 55% reported condomless heterosexual sex, 33% were men who had condomless sexual relations with other men and almost 13% were persons who inject drugs.

The cumulative incidence of seroconversions during 1989–2004 was 3.5%. The univariable analysis revealed that seroconversions were more frequent among males and that the frequency decreased with age. The incidence was higher among persons who inject drugs and MSM than among those having condomless heterosexual sex or sex workers. Regional differences in seroconversion rates were also observed. However, without a causal model, appropriate confounder control and further consideration of the implicit sampling mechanism, we cannot draw causal conclusions. Instead, the regression approach is interpreted rather as a predictive model, where the associations of variables are studied regardless of actual causal relations.

In this particular example where incidence is studied, Poisson regression or negative binomial regression are the recommended alternatives to, for instance, logistic regression, since these techniques enable the modelling of risk ratios. The predictive model can be used to predict the number of expected cases in relation to the risk factors, age and sex distribution of the participants in the different regions. This may allow for reasonable predictions, especially if the populations at the sites change over time. Please note that we did not explicitly consider potential multilevel aspects of the data. Similarly, we deliberately did not model potential time trends. Both these topics will be picked up again in the time series analysis (TSA) module.
